Loudness is the single most misunderstood part of getting a song ready to release. The old instinct
is "make it as loud as possible," but streaming changed the rules — and chasing peak loudness now
usually costs you more than it gains.

## What LUFS actually means

LUFS (Loudness Units relative to Full Scale) is a measurement of *perceived* loudness over time —
much closer to how loud something actually feels than a simple peak meter. It's the number that
matters because it's the number streaming platforms measure.

## Why "louder" stopped winning

Streaming services normalize playback to a target loudness. If your master comes in hotter than
their target, they turn it *down* on playback anyway — so all you're left with is the squashing and
distortion you added to get there, minus the loudness you were chasing.

- Spotify normalizes to roughly **-14 LUFS** (integrated)
- Apple Music targets around **-16 LUFS**
- YouTube lands near **-14 LUFS**

## A practical target

For most modern music, mastering somewhere in the **-14 to -9 LUFS** range (integrated) is a sane
window — leaning louder for dense, aggressive genres and quieter for dynamic, acoustic material.
The goal isn't to hit an exact number; it's to keep your dynamics intact while staying competitive.

- Leave true-peak headroom (aim for around **-1 dBTP**) so streaming codecs don't clip
- Reference against songs you love in the same genre, at matched loudness
- Trust your ears over the meter once you're in a reasonable range

## The short version

Don't master for a loudness war that ended years ago. Master for balance and dynamics, keep a little
headroom, and let the platforms handle normalization. If you'd like a second set of ears on where
your track is landing, that's exactly what [mastering](/services) — or a quick
[consultation](/contact) — is for.
